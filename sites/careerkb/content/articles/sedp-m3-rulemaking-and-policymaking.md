## Overview
OPM senior advisor Richard Loeb, a long-time career SES at OMB's Office of Federal Procurement Policy, explains how federal rules are made and challenged. He stresses that the APA is the main framework but not the only one, and that rules affect most executives' work.

## Key points
- Rules start from staff recommendations, congressional direction, or leadership agendas. [02:13]
- Procurement rules run under the OFPP Act rather than the APA [03:14]. Senior executives will probably be involved in digesting comments and building the rationale. [04:17]
- The twice-yearly Unified Agenda is the public's first formal notice of most rulemakings. [05:20]
- The APA requires at least 30 days of comment; EO 12866 favors 60, and complex rules often get 90 or more. [08:28]
- Electronic commenting sharply raised volume [11:30]; persuasive comments make cogent arguments backed by statute, case law, precedent and scholarly papers. [12:32]
- Final rules usually take effect 30 days after publication; some take years to complete. [15:40]
- Major rules face Congressional Review Act review [19:47], and some rules are enforced largely by private parties, such as bid protesters. [20:49]
- Loper Bright ended Chevron deference [23:56]; the speaker says its effects are still emerging. [24:57]

## Executive takeaways
- After Loper Bright, the speaker says, courts will still consider an agency's view, but agencies will have a tougher time justifying how they apply a rule. (ECQ1 Rule of Law)
- Plan the compliance approach while drafting, not after the rule is final. (ECQ5 Achieving Results)
- The speaker says rules will affect executives' work even when they are not directly involved; the twice-yearly Unified Agenda is where most planned rulemakings first appear. (ECQ5 Achieving Results)
