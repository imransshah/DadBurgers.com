## Overview
Charles Baldis, senior counsel at the Office of Special Counsel, finishes the tour of the Bill of Rights, focusing on non-criminal agency work. The course frames rights review as a design step, not a response to litigation.

## Key points
- Fourth Amendment analysis asks whether there is a reasonable expectation of privacy, then weighs it against the government's interest; it reaches administrative inspections and data privacy. [03:12]
- Sensitive data such as cell phone location history requires a warrant; for large data collections, Baldis advises anonymizing. [04:13]
- Due process means procedural fairness scaled to the interests at stake, covering benefit reductions, federal employee terminations and license revocations. [07:21]
- Affected people must be able to question and challenge an action before it is taken. [08:21]
- Takings include eminent domain at fair market value and physical intrusions such as government-caused flooding. [10:24]
- After SEC v. Jarkesy, in-house civil penalties resembling common-law suits need a jury unless the defendant consents; punitive penalties are likelier to trigger this. [13:27]
- The Excessive Fines Clause requires punitive sanctions to be proportional. [15:33]
- Federal agencies may incentivize or pay state officials to cooperate but cannot compel them. [19:39]

## Executive takeaways
- The course's practical step: add one rights checkpoint to the review of a policy, form, inspection protocol, data practice or public-facing workflow. (ECQ1 Rule of Law & Founding Principles)
- Rights review at design avoids litigation and rework later. (ECQ2 Driving Efficiency)
