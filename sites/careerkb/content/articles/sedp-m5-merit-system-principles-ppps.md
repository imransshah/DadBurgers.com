## Overview
Kim Steed, a Deputy Associate Director in OPM's Merit System Accountability and Compliance office, walks through the merit system principles (MSPs) and prohibited personnel practices (PPPs). She describes the MSPs as an ethos for management and the PPPs as guardrails.

## Key points
- The MSPs trace from the Garfield assassination and the Pendleton Act to the Civil Service Reform Act, which set out nine principles. [01:09]
- Fair treatment means judging applicants on knowledge, skills, and abilities. One agency's crediting plan kept qualified veterans off certificates until OPM intervened. [02:09]
- Performance management should set expectations in writing and produce consistent ratings across gender, location, and grade for similar work. [04:11]
- Training selection should follow clear, published criteria. [06:13]
- OPM evaluates agency HR operations and refers violations to the Office of Special Counsel. In one case, crediting only attorney experience for non-attorney jobs led to an investigation, required training, and penalties. [09:18]
- There are now 14 PPPs; nepotism appears often in evaluations. [12:26]
- On whistleblower reprisal risk, the speaker advises documenting everything and working with HR. [14:28]
- Discouraging employees from applying elsewhere obstructs competition. Recognition that favors headquarters over field staff can amount to favoritism; remote details can widen access. [16:31]
- Consequences range from a letter in the file to removal, plus organization-wide training for systemic problems. [20:38]

## Executive takeaways
- Objective, documented, job-related criteria in hiring, training, and awards are the main defense against PPPs. (ECQ3 Merit & Competence)
- The MSPs and PPPs are statutory limits on managerial discretion, and the workforce is expected to be apolitical. (ECQ1 Rule of Law & Founding Principles)
- In IT and operations groups split across sites or shifts, rotating high-profile work keeps recognition tied to results. (ECQ3 Merit & Competence)
