## The book in 60 seconds

- **What it is:** a short book (about 130 pages) built on McRaven's 2014 UT Austin commencement speech. Ten chapters, one lesson each, each anchored in a story from Navy SEAL training (BUD/S) or his career.
- **The thesis:** small disciplines, done daily, compound into the character that holds up when things go badly. Big change is many small acts.
- **Why a COO day uses it:** it is a shared vocabulary for execution, accountability, resilience and caring for the team. Nobody will quiz you on chapter 7; they will use the phrases.
- **If you only have 20 minutes:** watch the commencement speech. Same ten lessons, same stories.
- **If you only read two chapters:** 1 (Make your bed) and 10 (Never ring the bell). They frame the other eight.

## The lessons, for a Director and Deputy CIO

### 1. Start your day with a task completed

- **The idea:** "If you want to change the world, start off by making your bed." One finished task in the morning starts a chain of finished tasks, and if the day goes badly you still come home to a made bed.
- **For you:**
  - Your shop's "made bed" is basic hygiene: patching cadence, POA&M closure, timely ATOs, current asset inventory, answered data calls. Nobody gets credit for it, and everyone notices when it slips.
  - Model it. Start meetings on time, close the actions you own, answer the email you said you would. Teams copy the standard the leader actually keeps.
  - Use small wins on big modernization efforts. Ship something visible in the first 90 days so the program has momentum before it has critics.
- **Analogy:** soundcheck. Nobody buys a ticket for it, but a band that skips it sounds like it.

### 2. You can't go it alone

- **The idea:** a boat crew of seven only makes it through the surf if everyone paddles in rhythm. Find someone to help you paddle.
- **For you:**
  - IT outcomes depend on people you don't supervise: CFO, CAO/contracting, CHCO, the CISO, program owners, the COO. Build those relationships before you need them.
  - Your deputies and branch chiefs are the crew. Hire for complementary strengths, then trust them to paddle.
  - As Deputy CIO, part of the job is making the CIO's stroke and the team's stroke match.
- **Music:** a rhythm section. The best bassist in the world sounds lost if the drummer is somewhere else.

### 3. Only the size of your heart matters

- **The idea:** the crew of the shortest trainees, dismissed by everyone, outperformed the rest. Measure a person by the size of their heart.
- **For you:**
  - Talent hides in GS-9s, contractors, career changers and people without a CS degree. Look at drive and judgment, not just pedigree.
  - Use skills-based hiring and subject-matter-expert assessments where you can, and push back on résumé-keyword screening.
  - Sponsor people, don't just mentor them: put their name forward for details, briefings and stretch work.

### 4. Life's not fair. Drive on!

- **The idea:** trainees who passed inspection were sometimes made into a "sugar cookie" anyway: soaked in the surf, rolled in sand, made to carry it all day. The lesson: unfairness is part of the course.
- **For you:**
  - Continuing resolutions, hiring freezes, reorganizations, a sudden mandate with a 30-day deadline, a budget passback that cuts your top priority. Name it, absorb it, re-plan.
  - Don't let the team marinate in grievance. Acknowledge the hit in one meeting, then pivot to "here's what we control."
  - Protect morale by being honest about what you can't change and decisive about what you can.

### 5. Failure can make you stronger

- **The idea:** missing standards on the run earned you "the circus," two extra hours of punishing exercise. Over time the people who kept getting the circus got stronger than everyone else.
- **For you:**
  - Treat incidents, failed audits and missed milestones as data. Run blameless after-action reviews and fix the system, not the scapegoat.
  - An IG finding or a red FITARA grade is the circus: painful, public, and the fastest way to build real capability if you work it rather than spin it.
  - Tell your own failure stories. It gives your people permission to surface problems early.

### 6. You must dare greatly

- **The idea:** McRaven went down the "slide for life" head-first, the riskiest and fastest way, and set a course record. Sometimes you have to take the bold path.
- **For you:**
  - Decommission the legacy system. Push the cloud migration. Stand up the AI pilot with guardrails. Sunset the tool everyone loves and nobody uses.
  - "Dare" is not "reckless." Do the risk assessment, pick the controls, document the decision, then commit.
  - Federal culture rewards not failing. Your job is to make calculated risk safe for your people to take.

### 7. Stand up to the bullies

- **The idea:** during night swims, trainees were told that if a shark circled, hold your ground and punch it in the snout. Don't swim away.
- **For you:**
  - The "sharks" in a federal IT shop: a vendor missing SLAs, a stakeholder who bypasses governance, a shadow-IT purchase, a meeting where one voice steamrolls the room.
  - Hold the line on security, acquisition rules and your authorities, politely and in writing.
  - Stand up for your people when they are treated badly by others, including people senior to you.

### 8. Rise to the occasion

- **The idea:** on a night dive under a ship's hull, it is pitch black and disorienting. That is when you must be calm and use all your training.
- **For you:**
  - Your darkest moment is the major incident: ransomware, a breach notification, an outage during a mission-critical window, a congressional inquiry.
  - The team takes its emotional cue from you. Calm voice, clear roles, short updates, decisions on the clock.
  - Prepare before the dark: incident response plans, tabletop exercises, continuity plans, a call tree that works at 2 a.m.

### 9. Give people hope

- **The idea:** in Hell Week, neck-deep in freezing mud, one trainee started singing. Others joined, and the cold felt survivable. One person can change the world by giving people hope.
- **For you:**
  - During a long modernization, a hard reorganization, or a shutdown, people need to hear where this is going and why it's worth it.
  - Recognize people publicly and specifically. Hope is often just "someone noticed."
  - Be the steady, upbeat voice in the room when the room is tired. Optimism isn't denial; it's leadership.
- **Music:** "Lean on Me" or "Three Little Birds." Nobody's problems are solved by the song, but everybody's posture changes.

### 10. Never, ever quit

- **The idea:** any trainee could quit at any moment by ringing a brass bell. Many did. "Don't ever, ever ring the bell."
- **For you:**
  - Multi-year federal IT efforts outlast administrations, CIOs and budget cycles. Persistence is a core competency, not a personality trait.
  - Don't let your people ring the bell either. Notice burnout early: workload, recognition, telework flexibility, EAP.
  - Know the difference between quitting and re-scoping. Killing a failing project is courage, not quitting.

## Training day talking points

- **Your one-liner:** "The book is about the unglamorous stuff: small disciplines, teamwork, and staying steady when it's unfair. That's most of government IT."
- **Lesson most relevant to IT:** 1 (make your bed) maps to cyber hygiene and operational discipline. 8 (rise to the occasion) maps to incident response.
- **Lesson most relevant to a COO:** 2 (you can't go it alone). IT, finance, HR, acquisition and facilities either paddle together or the boat flips.
- **A personal example to bring:** one time you were "sugar-cookied" (a decision outside your control that hurt your team) and how you drove on.
- **A question to ask the room:** "What's our organization's 'made bed', the small standard we keep every day that tells everyone we're serious?"
- **A commitment to offer:** one small daily discipline you'll model for the next 90 days.

## Discussion questions you may get

- Which lesson is hardest for you personally? (Have one ready; 4 and 6 are the most honest answers for most leaders.)
- Where does our culture punish daring (lesson 6) and how do we fix that?
- Who in your organization is the "munchkin crew" (lesson 3) that's being overlooked?
- How do we give people hope (lesson 9) during budget and workforce uncertainty?
