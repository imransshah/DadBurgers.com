## Overview
OPM senior advisor Richard Loeb, a long-time career SES at OMB's Office of Federal Procurement Policy, explains how federal rules are made and challenged. He stresses that the APA is the main framework but not the only one, and that rules affect most executives' work.

## Key points
- Rules start from staff recommendations, congressional direction, or leadership agendas. [01:09]
- Procurement rules run under the OFPP Act rather than the APA. Executives mostly shape rules by digesting comments and writing the rationale. [03:14]
- The twice-yearly Unified Agenda is the public's first formal notice of most rulemakings. [05:20]
- The APA requires at least 30 days of comment; EO 12866 favors 60, and complex rules often get 90 or more. [08:28]
- Electronic commenting sharply raised volume; persuasive comments cite statute, case law and research. [11:30]
- Final rules usually take effect 30 days after publication; some take years to complete. [15:40]
- Major rules face Congressional Review Act review, and some rules are enforced largely by private parties, such as bid protesters. [18:45]
- Loper Bright ended Chevron deference; the speaker says its effects are still emerging. [23:56]

## Executive takeaways
- After Loper Bright, a clear legal basis and stated rationale matter more than deference. (ECQ1 Rule of Law)
- Plan the compliance approach while drafting, not after the rule is final. (ECQ5 Achieving Results)
- Rule effective dates drive system and workflow changes; tracking the Unified Agenda gives IT operations lead time. (ECQ5 Achieving Results)
