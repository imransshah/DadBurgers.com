## Overview
OPM Director Scott Kupor, a few months into his first public-sector role after careers in investment banking, a startup and venture capital, is interviewed by an OPM career executive. His central argument is that people respond to the incentives an organization creates, so changing federal workforce behavior starts with objectives and incentive design.

## Key points
- Public and private objectives differ, but objectives drive incentives in both; he attributes most behavior problems to the system rather than to the people. [08:04]
- He sees no distinct federal personality type; most staff want to do the right thing, and the task is removing obstacles. [09:05]
- He favors two-way movement between sectors [11:06] and argues tenure-based pay and promotion proxies hold back strong early-career staff [14:08]; exit and re-entry should be as seamless as possible. [21:14]
- On hiring, he recommends people who learn fast ("hire athletes," Kupor) over exact prior experience [18:12], drawn from beyond the current federal workforce. [19:13]
- Government leadership relies on persuasion: he says there is no single decision maker, and he must convince career staff, other agencies, the White House and Congress. [27:19]
- Workforce planning starts from mission, objectives and metrics; headcount follows [29:21], after asking what to stop doing and whether technology, process or organizational change are alternatives to hiring. [30:22]
- New staffing plans tie annual hiring to budget and objectives, with quarterly reporting to OPM and OMB [32:23]; deviations are allowed if explained. [34:24]
- Succession practice: quarterly career conversations, candid feedback, and small management assignments for individual contributors. [38:28]
- He wants real rating dispersion; bonuses spread evenly lose incentive value [42:32], and recognition should be individualized to how each person wants to receive it. [44:35]

## Executive takeaways
- Kupor treats headcount as derived from mission and objectives, with technology, process or organizational change weighed as alternatives to hiring. (ECQ2 Driving Efficiency)
- Kupor describes convincing people outside one's managerial control as the central leadership test in government. (ECQ4 Leading People)
- Kupor argues for progression tied to skills rather than tenure, and for ratings and rewards that differentiate contribution. (ECQ3 Merit & Competence)
- He argues people overestimate downside risk and underestimate upside, and that executives who take an appropriate degree of risk should be rewarded. [49:39] (ECQ2 Driving Efficiency)
