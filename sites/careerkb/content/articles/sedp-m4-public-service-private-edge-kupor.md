## Overview
OPM Director Scott Kupor, a few months into his first public-sector role after careers in investment banking, a startup and venture capital, is interviewed by an OPM career executive. His central argument is that people respond to the incentives an organization creates, so changing federal workforce behavior starts with objectives and incentive design.

## Key points
- Public and private objectives differ, but objectives drive incentives in both; he attributes most behavior problems to the system rather than to the people. [08:04]
- He sees no distinct federal personality type; most staff want to do the right thing, and the task is removing obstacles. [09:05]
- He favors two-way movement between sectors, with easy exit and re-entry, and argues tenure-based pay and promotion proxies hold back strong early-career staff. [14:08]
- On hiring, he recommends people who learn fast ("hire athletes," Kupor) over exact prior experience, drawn from beyond the current federal workforce. [18:12]
- Government leadership relies on persuasion, since no single decision maker controls career staff, other agencies, the White House or Congress. [27:19]
- Workforce planning starts from mission, objectives and metrics; headcount follows, after asking what to stop doing and whether technology, process or structure could substitute. [29:21]
- New staffing plans tie annual hiring to budget and objectives, with quarterly check-ins with OPM and OMB; deviations are allowed if explained. [32:23]
- Succession practice: quarterly career conversations, candid feedback, and small management assignments for individual contributors. [38:28]
- He wants real rating dispersion; bonuses spread evenly lose incentive value, and recognition works best when individualized. [42:32]

## Executive takeaways
- Headcount requests are stronger when derived from objectives and paired with a process or automation alternative. (ECQ2 Driving Efficiency)
- Influence without formal authority is a core federal executive skill. (ECQ4 Leading People)
- Progression and rewards tied to demonstrated skill and contribution, rather than tenure, support a merit-based workforce. (ECQ3 Merit & Competence)
- He argues executives overestimate the downside of reasoned, well-documented risk. [48:39] (ECQ2 Driving Efficiency)
